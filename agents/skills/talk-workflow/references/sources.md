# Sources and rulings

The reasoning behind the load-bearing rules, plus the citation table. Load only when you need to defend a rule or check a source. This is supporting depth, not the coaching itself - the coaching lives in `SKILL.md`.

## Provenance honesty

The whole framework depends on one discipline: **a citation is either verified or it is flagged as craft, and the two are never mixed.** The moment the skill quotes "Miller's 7+/-2" as a design allowance, it has committed exactly the laundering it exists to prevent.

Three tiers, strongest first:

- **Verified primary** - a source was fetched and read this research round.
- **Verified secondary** - multiple independent summaries agree on the claim, but no primary document was read.
- **Recalled / practitioner-craft** - carried over from memory or industry practice, with no controlled evidence. Used only where the downside risk is near zero, and always labeled as such.

Two rules follow:

1. Where sources disagreed on *how deeply* a claim was verified, defer to the one that read the primary source.
2. Anything recalled or practitioner-craft is stated as craft in the guidance. Never dress it up as research.

## The load-bearing rulings

### Ruling 1 - Assertion-Evidence is the default; Zen minimalism is a per-slide exception at four named positions

The two schools agree on the slide *body* - both ban bullet lists, both want a single visual. They disagree on exactly one thing: the full-sentence headline. AE wins the default for three reasons. First, Garner & Alley 2013 (n=110) is the only controlled comparison in the entire evidence base that measures the outcomes that actually matter - comprehension and delayed recall - and it favors AE over the bulleted default. Second, the success criterion is an audience retaining takeaways: a sentence headline is a persistent, photographable, note-takeable artifact of the claim, and a wordless slide gives them nothing to write down. Third, robustness: Zen pushes 100% of the semantic load onto delivery and fails hard when delivery is merely competent, whereas AE degrades gracefully. A reusable default should be the one that survives a mediocre run.

Zen applies at exactly four positions, capped at 3 wordless slides in a 30-minute talk: the opening hook, the emotional/STAR moment, section transitions, and the closing image. These are the primacy/recency and emotional-encoding slots. Anywhere else, a wordless slide means the assertion is not written yet.

### Ruling 5 - Chunk limit: never "7+/-2". <=3 visual chunks, <=40 words total

Miller (1956) measured immediate serial recall of unrelated items with no competing task. A slide audience is dual-tasking - listening while looking - and needs comprehension, not list recall. Quoting 7 as a design allowance roughly doubles the budget against a number that never applied to this situation.

Cowan 2001 puts the limit at 3-5 chunks, averaging ~4, under conditions that prevent rehearsal and chunking. That is a ceiling under ideal focus, not a design target for a distracted listener, so the design number sits below it: 3.

State it honestly in one sentence, or it gets re-laundered downstream: *"the ceiling is about four chunks under ideal focus (Cowan 2001); a listening audience is worse than ideal; therefore design below four."*

### Ruling 8 - Human gates: exactly one, at angle/title. Recall-phrase wording is human-owned everywhere

The gate is hard-gated at angle/title because the angle determines structure, tone, which concepts surface, and every downstream slide - an automated choice produces a generic talk. It is the highest-leverage, lowest-cost place to spend human judgment.

One gate, not several, because gates that fire everywhere get clicked through and stop being gates. The one carve-out: the *wording* of the 2-3 recall-phrases is authored by the speaker, because those are the sentences being memorized, repeated verbatim three times, and delivered under stress - they have to sound like the speaker or the whole cycling mechanism reads as recited marketing copy.

### Ruling 10 - Content freeze at T-3

Spacing governs retrieval reps (recalling the talk); freshness governs content edits (changing the talk). They are not in conflict - they govern different objects. The freeze date resolves it: after T-3, no edits to slides, script, or structure, only reps. A night-before edit invalidates every rep already done - the speaker delivers a talk they have never rehearsed.

### Ruling 12 - The delayed recall check is mandatory

Feedback forms and same-day reactions measure *satisfaction*. The success criterion is *retention*, which is by definition a claim about delayed memory. Asking 2-3 attendees a week later "what do you remember from that talk?" is the only cheap measurement that actually tests it - and it is the audience-side application of the same testing effect the rehearsal phase is built on.

Making it mandatory is the difference between a framework that is evidence-grounded and one that merely cites evidence: without it, every claim is unfalsifiable in practice.

### Ruling 14 - "Rule of three": not stated as a rule

Folk wisdom, not principle. The skill already lands on 2-3 takeaways from the success criterion and the working-memory argument, so nothing is gained by dressing it up - and doing so is the kind of laundering this discipline exists to prevent. Say "2-3, because that is what a listening audience retains and what you can repeat three times each," and drop the numerology.
