## Phase 1 - Harvest (extracting the idea from the mumble)

- "Describe your subject in detail. What is your topic? What are its components? What are its interesting and distinguishing features? What are its puzzles? Distinguish your subject from those that are similar to it. How is your subject unlike others?" - UNC Writing Center, *Brainstorming* handout
- "What is the history of your subject? How has it changed over time? Why? What are the significant events that have influenced your subject?" - UNC Writing Center, *Brainstorming*
- "What is your subject related to? What is it influenced by? How? What does it influence? How? Who has a stake in your topic? Why? What fields do you draw on for the study of your subject? Why? How has your subject been approached by others? How is their work related to yours?" - UNC Writing Center, *Brainstorming*
- "Look over what you've written. Do any of the responses suggest anything new about your topic? What interactions do you notice among the 'sides'? That is, do you see patterns repeating, or a theme emerging that you could use to approach the topic or draft a thesis?" - UNC Writing Center, *Brainstorming*
- "After you have produced a list of options, look over your ideas. What kinds of ideas come forward? What patterns or associations do you find?" - UNC Writing Center, *Brainstorming*
- "Tell me about what you're writing." - `story-coach` (jwynia), MIT
- "What specifically feels stuck?" - `story-coach` (jwynia), MIT
- "What have you tried so far?" - `story-coach` (jwynia), MIT
- "If you could only keep one element, what stays?" - `story-coach` (jwynia), MIT
- "What would surprise even you about where this goes?" - `story-coach` (jwynia), MIT
- Center-of-gravity prompt (paraphrased from Elbow 1973, per j-vaught notes): identify "the main point, feeling, or energy around which the piece implicitly organizes itself" - i.e. ask the writer: *where is the energy? what does this writing actually care about, as opposed to what you think it should care about?* **[paraphrase, not verbatim - Elbow's term "center of gravity" is verbatim]**
- "Why should I think that?" (McEnerney's replacement for "why do you think that?") - McEnerney lecture transcript, t188-190
- "What's the difference between 'and' and 'but' in creating value?" - McEnerney lecture transcript, t319-320
- "So what?" / "Why should anybody care?" (the conclusion drill) - UNC Writing Center, *Conclusions* handout
- Berke's Twenty Questions for the Writer (20 thought-starters, each mapping to an essay genre), verbatim from Purdue OWL *More Prewriting Questions* ("Adapted from Jacqueline Berke's *Twenty Questions for the Writer*"):

  - "What are the component parts of X? (Simple Analysis)"

  - "How should X be made or done? (Directional Analysis)"
  - "What is the essential function of X? (Functional Analysis)"
  - "What are the causes of X? (Causal Analysis)"
  - "What are the consequences of X? (Causal Analysis)"
  - "What are the types of X? (Classification)"
  - "How is X like or unlike Y? (Comparison)"
  - "What is the present status of X? (Comparison)"
  - "What is the significance of X? (Interpretation)"
  - "What are the facts about X? (Reportage)"
  - "How did X happen? (Narration)"
  - "What kind of person is X? (Characterization/Profile)"
  - "What is my personal response to X? (Reflection)"
  - "What is my memory of X? (Reminiscence)"
  - "What is the value of X? (Evaluation)"
  - "What are the essential major points or features of X? (Summary)"
  - "What case can be made for or against X? (Persuasion)"

## Phase 3 - Spine (structure)

- "How am I ordering ideas? Can I describe the pattern?" / "Why are the ideas presented in this order? Would they make more sense if I reorder them?" / "What effect does the order of ideas have on my readers?" / "How would reordering the information affect my paper?" - UNC Writing Center, *Flow* handout
- "What is the single most important thing this communication is saying? Is it in the first sentence, subject line, or first slide? If not, that is problem one." - Minto Pyramid Audit, via DogInfantry/claude-skill-management-consultant-B1 `pyramid-principle-and-scqa.md`
- "Would removing one reason leave the governing thought unsupported?" - Minto Pyramid Audit, ibid.
- "Can you read the section titles / slide titles in sequence and follow a clear argument?" - Minto Pyramid Audit, ibid.
- "If a reader only saw the title and the chart, would they know what action to take?" - Minto Pyramid Audit, ibid.
- "Lead with your recommendation first - what is your governing thought?" (coaching a case candidate) - Minto SCQA reference, ibid.
- SITUATION / COMPLICATION / QUESTION / ANSWER framing: "What is true now? (shared context - not news to the audience)" / "What has changed or what's the tension? (why this matters now)" / "What does that make the audience want to know? (implicit 'so what' made explicit)" - Minto SCQA, ibid.
- "One paragraph equals one reader question... write the question the reader has at that point. The topic sentence should answer, frame, or narrow that question." - Reader-Aware-Writing, `cohesion-coherence.md` (MIT)
- "What is the heart of this assignment, what's the main thing I need to do to respond? Is there a central question I need to answer?" - UW-Madison Writer's Handbook, *Interpreting Writing Assignments*
- "What kind (or genre) of paper do I need to write? How much do I know about this kind of paper?" - UW-Madison Writer's Handbook, *Interpreting Writing Assignments*
- "Are there sub-questions I need to answer?" - UW-Madison Writer's Handbook, *Interpreting Writing Assignments*
- "What kind of evidence do I need to base my arguments or analysis on?" - UW-Madison Writer's Handbook, *Interpreting Writing Assignments*
- "What are the different parts of this paper?" - UW-Madison Writer's Handbook, *Interpreting Writing Assignments*
- Peer-review ordering: "Comment on large issues first (Does the draft respond to the assignment? Are important and interesting ideas presented? Is the main point clear and interesting? Is there a clear focus? Is the draft effectively organized? Is the sequence of points logical? Are ideas adequately developed? If appropriate, is the draft convincing in its argument? Is evidence used properly?). Go on to smaller issues later (awkward or confusing sentences, style, grammar, word choice, proofreading)." - UW-Madison Writer's Handbook, *Revising Your Paper / peer review*
- "Place gold coins along the path. Reward the reader with high points, especially in the middle." - Roy Peter Clark, *Writing Tools*, Tool 32 (via mgp/book-notes)

## Phase 4 - Draft (the silent phase)

This phase is silent unless asked. When the writer asks for help, the move is Elbow's: ask what they are trying to say in the paragraph, in their own words, badly. Do not produce prose.

- "What are you trying to say in this paragraph? Say it out loud, badly." - Elbow move (paraphrased by the brief, not the bank) **[mine adaptation of Elbow's freewriting prompt]**
- "What were you trying to do here, and what does the reader now believe that they didn't before this paragraph?" - session-pattern prompt from the SKILL.md **[mine]**
- "What would she say if she finally admitted the truth?" / "What image or moment could open this scene?" / "Try writing just the first line of that scene." / "Describe what he notices when he walks in." (turn a sentence-level fix into a writing prompt) - `story-coach` (jwynia), MIT

## Phase 5 - Interrogate (revision-pass-gated questions)

Order matches the gate: structure → paragraph coherence → sentence clarity → concision → rhythm → proofread. Do not raise a lower-pass question while a higher pass is still wrong.

- "Do my sentences make sense individually? How's my writing style?" - UNC Writing Center, *Getting Feedback* (draft-stage question set)
- "Are the transitions between my ideas smooth and effective?" - UNC Writing Center, *Getting Feedback*
- "Underline or list the first five to six words of each sentence. Check whether those openings form a related set of topics." (audit, not question) - Reader-Aware-Writing, `cohesion-coherence.md` (MIT)
- "What's the difference between 'and' and 'but' in creating value?" - McEnerney lecture transcript, t319-320
- "Never let your explanations be the end of the matter - always have students demonstrate their understanding of a concept." (principle, not question) - Purdue OWL, *Strategies for Tutoring Grammar*
- "At no point should the tutor do the work of proofreading for the student... they should always ensure that it is the work of the tutee, not the tutor, which produces results." (the writing-centre credo, quoted verbatim) - Purdue OWL, *Strategies for Tutoring Grammar*

## Phase 6 - Log (the improvement loop)

- "How should I interpret the comments on my paper? Why did I receive the grade I did? What else might I have done to strengthen this paper? What can I learn as a writer about this writing experience? What should I do the next time I have to write a paper?" - UNC Writing Center, *Getting Feedback*
- "Do I have a point? Does my argument make sense? Is it logical and consistent? Is it supported by sufficient evidence?" - UNC Writing Center, *Getting Feedback*
- "On a sentence level, take note of which errors you make frequently." (the personal error log, in embryo) - Purdue OWL, *Finding Common Errors*
- "Do you feel more confident about tackling this revision now?" (conference wrap-up) - Purdue OWL Writing Lab, *The Writing Conference* presentation
- "What questions do you have about the assignment?" / "What obstacles are you encountering with your writing?" (conference opener) - Purdue OWL Writing Lab, *The Writing Conference*
- "What changed in your understanding from where you started?" / "Can you summarize what you've discovered in your own words?" / "How would you explain this to someone who hasn't read the document?" (synthesis state) - oakreader `socratic` skill, MIT
- "What are you assuming when you say that? Does the document support that assumption?" / "If the author is right about this, what else must be true?" / "Why do you think I asked that question? What was I trying to help you see?" (assumption / implication / meta toolkit) - oakreader `socratic` skill, MIT
- "Would you like me to point you to the specific section that addresses this, or would you like a hint about the direction?" (the aporia safety valve, offered only after three unanswered questions) - oakreader `socratic` skill, MIT
- "What does the error message say?" / "Can you explain that back to me in your own words?" (the socratic-tutor stock questions) - `socratic-tutor` (claude-skill-registry), MIT
- "Be a guide, not a 'guru': help the student to discover his/her own methods and ideas." / "Model, don't prescribe: demonstrate how to apply something once, then let the student invent on his/her own." - Purdue OWL Writing Lab, *The Writing Conference*
- Deliberate-practice design questions (self-coached writer): "a drill is only worth doing if its feedback is objective, because a self-coached writer cannot supply a judgement they do not already have... Prefer drills whose result you can read off." - supermarmar/guides, lecture 6
- Descriptive-feedback prompt: "Do not ask whether it was clear. Ask where they slowed down, what they thought it was about, and what they would do about it." - supermarmar/guides, lecture 5
- Revision-pass gate: pass zero asks "whether anything in the writing sent you back to the data" - supermarmar/guides, lecture 4
- "[mine] What's the one thing you said that you didn't know you knew?" (the only shape of question the sources leave open; example of a genuinely new extraction question) - **[mine]**
